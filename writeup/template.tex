\section{Variational neural network for the committor problem.}

Consider the overdamped Langevin dynamics
\begin{equation}\label{eq:overdamped_langevin}
  dx(t) = - \nabla V(x(t)) \, dt + \sqrt{2 \beta^{-1}} dw(t)
\end{equation}
and two disjoint sets \(A\) and \(B\) containing minima of \(V\).
The committor \(q(x)\) is the probability that the process starting at \(x\)
will reach \(B\) before \(A\).
Of interest for solving with a neural network is the variational formulation,
\(q\) is the minimizer of the functionl
\begin{equation}\label{eq:committor_functional}
  I[u] = \int_{\Omega_{AB}} e^{-\beta V(x)} \abs{\nabla u(x)}^2 \, dx
\end{equation}
over all \(u \in C^2(\Omega_{AB}) \cap C(\closure{\Omega_{AB}})\),
satisfying boundary conditions \(u(\bdy A) = 0\), \(u(\bdy B) = 1\) \cite{face_nn_deltanet}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Rugged Mueller potential}
The rugged Mueller potential is a 10D variant of the Mueller potential, a common 2D test problem.
As described in \cite{yuan2024}, the potential is given by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ruggedmuellerpot}
  V(x) = V_0(x_1, x_2) + \gamma \sin(2 k \pi x_1) \sin(2 k \pi x_2) + \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{i=3}^{10} x_i^2
\end{equation}
where \(x \in \R^{10}\), and 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:muellerpot}
  V_0(x_1, x_2)  = \sum_{i=1}^4 D_i \exp\left(a_i (x_1 - X_i)^2 + b_i (x_1 - X_i)(x_2-Y_i) + c_i (x_2 - Y_i)^2\right)
\end{equation}
Parameter values are taken from \cite{yuan2024}, and are given in Table~\ref{tab:muellerparams}.
\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Parameters of the rugged Mueller potential \eqref{eq:ruggedmuellerpot}--\eqref{eq:muellerpot}.}
  \label{tab:muellerparams}
  \begin{tabular}{c|rrrr}
    \(i\) & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
    \hline
    \(a_i\) & \(-1\)   & \(-1\)   & \(-6.5\) & \(0.7\) \\
    \(b_i\) & \(0\)    & \(0\)    & \(11\)   & \(0.6\) \\
    \(c_i\) & \(-10\)  & \(-10\)  & \(-6.5\) & \(0.7\) \\
    \(D_i\) & \(-200\) & \(-100\) & \(-170\) & \(15\)  \\
    \(X_i\) & \(1\)    & \(0\)    & \(-0.5\) & \(-1\)  \\
    \(Y_i\) & \(0\)    & \(0.5\)  & \(1.5\)  & \(1\)   \\
    \hline
    \multicolumn{5}{c}{\(\gamma = 9, \quad k = 5, \quad \sigma = 0.05\)}
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}
The metadynamics algorithm additionally requires the gradient of the potential.
Letting 
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \delta X_j &= (x_1 - X_j), \\
    \qquad \delta Y_j &= (x_2 - Y_j),\\
    \qquad S_j &= a_j (\delta X_j)^2 + b_j (\delta X_j)(\delta Y_j) + c_j(\delta Y_j)^2
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
the gradient is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grad_mueller_potential}
  \begin{aligned}
    (\nabla V(x))_1 &= 
      \sum_{j=1}^4 \Big( D_j \exp(S_j) \left(2 a_j (\delta X_j) + b_j (\delta Y_j)\right) \Big)
      + \gamma 2 \pi k \cos(2k \pi x_1) \sin(2 k \pi x_2)\\
    (\nabla V(x))_2 &= 
      \sum_{j=1}^4 \Big( D_j \exp(S_j) \left(b_j (\delta X_j) + 2 c_j (\delta Y_j)\right) \Big)
      + \gamma 2 \pi k \sin(2k \pi x_1) \cos(2 k \pi x_2)\\
    (\nabla V(x))_i &= \frac{2}{\sigma^2} x_i \qquad \text{for } i \ge 3
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
An efficient vectorized implementation of both the potential and the gradient is given in
\href{https://github.com/jrippeon/amsc665project2/blob/main/src/committor/mueller.py}{\texttt{mueller.py}}.

\subsection{Euler-Maruyama simulation}
In the metadynamics algorithm (as described in \cite{make_training_data_face}),
the Euler-Maruyama algorithm is used to simulate overdamped Langevin dynamics,
given by \eqref{eq:overdamped_langevin}.
An Euler-Maruyama step of length \(h\) is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:overdamped_langevin_euler_maruyama}
  x_{n+1} = x_n - \nabla V(x_n) h + \sqrt{2 \beta^{-1}} w
\end{equation}
where \(w \sim \mathcal N(0, h)\).
This is implemented in 
\href{https://github.com/jrippeon/amsc665project2/blob/main/src/committor/metadynamics.py}{\texttt{metadynamics.py}},
as \texttt{euler\_maruyama()}.

\subsection{Metadynamics sampling}
To train our neural network, we need a representative collection of points,
which are sufficiently dense near the important features of our potential to capture the details of the committor.
In a (relatively) high-dimensional problem such as this 10D Mueller, a grid will not suffice,
so we use a sampling method informed by the dynamics.

The metadynamics algorithm aims to slowly ``fill up'' the potential wells in our system until a region of interest is roughly flat,
then sample randomly from this region.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
The metadynamics sampling algorithm has two phases:
a deposition phase, and a sampling phase.

In the deposition phase (\texttt{metadynamics\_deposition()}),
we send an Euler-Maruyama trajectory through the potential landscape,
periodically depositing Guassian ``bumps''.
These fill potential wells, flattening the landscape and allowing more uniform sampling.

These Gaussian ``bumps'' live in a lower-dimensional space of ``collective variables''.
We consider a map \(\xi : \R^d \to \R^m\) to the space of collective variables,
and let 
\begin{equation}
  B_k(x) = h_k \exp \left(-\frac{\abs{\xi(x)- \xi(x_k)}^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right)
\end{equation}
where \(h_k\) is the height of the \(k\)-th bump (constant in our case).

Once the potential landscape is flattened in this way, 
we begin the sampling phase (\texttt{metadynamics\_sampling()}).
To sample, we run more Euler-Maruyama trajectories, 
collecting a point every \(N_\text{skip}\) steps. 
This set of points is then thinned using the \(\delta\)-net algorithm,
which basically consists of iterating through the points in order and at each step deleting all points with distance \(\delta\) of the point under consideration.
The remaining points then constitute the training set.
This is the approach used in \cite{make_training_data_face}.

I introduce one optimization in 
\href{https://github.com/jrippeon/amsc665project2/blob/main/src/committor/metadynamics.py}{\texttt{metadynamics.py}},
and Claude helpfully introduced still greater optimization in 
\href{https://github.com/jrippeon/amsc665project2/blob/main/src/committor/metadynamics_optimized.py}{\texttt{metadynamics\_optimized.py}}.
This brings the runtime from the (estimated, I never actually ran it all the way) 40 minute range down to a minute or so.

My contribution was to recognize that the sampling phase (longest by far) could be modified to take advantage of \texttt{numpy} vectorization,
running a large number of Euler-Maruyama trajectories forward simultaneously.
This doesn't technically match the reference implementation, but it's cheap to implement so I did it.
As long as the total amount of time which passes is long I don't think it will result in large differences in the sample. 

Claude's contribution was to precompute the gradient contribution from the bumps on a uniform grid in collective variable space,
and determine the gradient during sampling by linear interpolation.
This is not too expensive, because the bumps are in the two dimensional collective variable space,
rather than the full 10 dimensional space.
The gradient is given by
\begin{equation}
  -\nabla (V(x_n) + \sum_{j=1}^{N_\text{bumps}}) B_j
\end{equation}
and must be computed at each Euler-Maruyama step.
This change replaces a sum of length \(N_\text{bumps}\), by a single linear interpolation.

An obvious optimization to the \(\delta\)-net algorithm is to use a KD tree.
This is a data structure which partitions \(k\)-dimensional space (in this case the 2-dimensional CV space),
repeatedly in a tree structure.
Nearest neighbor search is then extremely fast.
Claude's implementation uses \texttt{scipy.spacial.cKDTree.query\_ball\_point()},
which is significantly faster than the naive implementation.

\subsection{Neural Network}

\newcommand{\network}{\mathcal{N}}

Now that we have sampled a representative collection of points,
we can train a network to minimize \eqref{eq:committor_functional}.
The loss function is a discrete approximation of this functional, given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:committor_loss}
  \operatorname{Loss} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \abs{\nabla \network(x_k ; \theta)}^2 \frac{e^{- \beta V(x_x)}}{\rho(x_k)}
\end{equation}
where \(\rho(x_k)\) is the density of samples near \(x_k\).
The sum over only training points is a reasonable approximation of the integral due to our sampling technique.
The metadynamics method fills potential wells up to some (rather high) potential, then samples mostly from the area below that level.
The areas which are rarely sampled by this method are those with \(V\) much higher than the ``floor'' of our flattened potential,
so the \(e^{-\beta V(x)}\) term will be essentially zero in these locations.
Because of the \(\delta\)-net refinement, we can assume samples are uniformly distributed and factor out the constant \(\rho\),
giving the expression used in \texttt{loss\_fn()} in 
\href{https://github.com/jrippeon/amsc665project2/blob/main/src/committor/committor_model.py}{\texttt{committor\_model.py}}.

\subsubsection{Boundary conditions}

While we could enforce the boundary conditions in our loss function,
in this case it is possible (and therefore preferable) to enforce them analytically.

We will train a model \(\network\) such that 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:committor_bc_model}
  q(x) = (1 - \chi_A(x)) ((1 - \chi_B(x)) \network(x; \theta) + \chi_B(x))
\end{equation}
where \(\chi_A\) and \(\chi_B\) are smoothed approximations to the indicator functions of \(A\) and \(B\) respectively \cite{li2019, cameron_nns4pdes}.
This frees the model from having to learn the boundary conditions, 
and should lead to easier and more accurate training.
In the case of the rugged Mueller problem described in \cite{yuan2024},
\(A = B_r(a)\) and \(B=B_r(b)\), so I choose to implement these as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:smooth_indicator}
  \chi_A(x) = \frac12 - \frac12 \tanh\left(
    \frac{2 \alpha}{2 r w + w^2} \abs{x - a}^2 - \alpha - \frac{2 \alpha r^2}{2 r w + w^2}
  \right)
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha\) is some value beyond which \(s(x) = \frac12 - \frac12 \tanh(x)\) is ``essentially zero'' or ``essentially one'' (\(\alpha = 4\) in my implementation)
and \(w\) is the width of the transition region. 
Thus if \(\abs{x-a} = r\) then \(\chi_A(x) = s(-\alpha) \approx 1\),
and if \(\abs{x-a} = r+w\) then \(\chi_A(x) = s(\alpha) \approx 0\).

\subsection{Verifying the results}
Due to the high dimensionality of the problem,
it is not feasible to use an FEM solution to verify the model output as was the case with \cite{face_nn_deltanet}.
Instead we use the committor analysis approach discussed in \cite{li2019,project2}.
In this method, we extract the isocommittor surface \(q = 0.5\) in \((x_1, x_2)\) (where the interest behavior is),
sample \(N\) points on it according to \(e^{-\beta V(x)}\).
From these points we can launch \(M\) trajectories, and count the number that end up in both \(A\) and \(B\).
From this we can estimate the value of the committor at each of the points,
and plot a histogram, which should ideally be centered at 0.5.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
The necessary functions for this procedure are implemented (by Claude) in 
\href{https://github.com/jrippeon/amsc665project2/blob/main/src/committor/committor_validation.py}{\texttt{committor\_validation.py}},
and called in
\href{https://github.com/jrippeon/amsc665project2/blob/main/src/committor/committor_results.py}{\texttt{committor\_results.py}},
The procedure is conducted as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{committor\_results.py}:
  The committor is evaluated on a grid in the 2-dimensional CV space,
  giving an array of \(q(x_1, x_2, 0, \dotsc, 0)\).
  \item \texttt{level\_curve()}: 
  The \(q = 0.5\) level curve is extracted using \texttt{contourpy.contour\_generator()} (designed for this purpose).
  The longest arc of the result is extracted (to avoid possible disconnected components).
  \item \texttt{resample\_arclength()}: 
  Convert the result from a polyline to a sequence of discrete,
  uniformly spaced points.
  \item \texttt{sample\_on\_curve()}:
  Sample from these points according to \(e^{-\beta V(x)}\).
  The results of this are shown in \autoref{fig:isocommittor_surface}.
  \item Take these coordinates in CV space and translate them to the full 10D space.
  Claude suggested perturbing them according to the known quadratic potential in the additional dimensions,
  rather than simply letting \(x_3 = \dotsb = x_{10} = 0\).
  \item \texttt{shooting\_committtor()}: Run the trajectories forward and see where they end up.
\end{enumerate}


The histogram in \autoref{fig:committor_histogram} shows the results of this experiment.
The network consistently overestimates the value of the committor, 
with the (estimated) true values on the \(\network(x; \theta) = 1/2\) surface
lying well below \(1/2\), with remarkably little spread.

This error was consistent across model parameter and hyperparameter changes.
Regenerating a different training / test set,
decreasing the width of \eqref{eq:smooth_indicator}, 
and increasing the size of the network to \(5 \times 15\),
all failed to meaningfully increase the sample mean,
so I gave up.
